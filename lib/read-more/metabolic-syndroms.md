
Metabolic Syndrome:
Pathophysiology, Epidemiology in Africa, and A Strategic Review of Pharmacological and Complementary Therapeutics

Metabolic Syndrome Crisis
Metabolic Syndrome (MS) is a complex cluster of interrelated metabolic and physiological abnormalities. These disorders, when clustered, substantially increase an individual's lifetime risk for developing chronic conditions, including Type 2 Diabetes Mellitus (T2DM) and premature Cardiovascular Disease (CVD), notably ischemic heart disease.1 The historical evolution of this concept has moved the clinical focus beyond treating individual risk factors—such as hyperglycemia, dyslipidemia, and hypertension—to recognizing the synergistic and multiplicative effects of their co-occurrence. This clustering underscores the syndrome's multi-systemic nature, necessitating a holistic and integrated therapeutic approach.1

Pathophysiological Drivers: 
The core pathogenesis of Metabolic Syndrome is centered on the interplay between Insulin Resistance (IR) and chronic, low-grade inflammation. Insulin is defined by the weakened physiological effect of insulin in the body, where target cells, primarily in the skeletal muscles, liver, and adipose tissues, become less sensitive to the hormone. Consequently, relatively greater amounts of insulin are required to maintain a state of normal blood glucose level.5 A secondary, yet essential, component driving MS is visceral obesity.1 Adipose tissue, particularly visceral fat, is not merely a passive energy storage depot but functions actively as a dynamic endocrine organ5 by secreting numerous proinflammatory factors known as adipokines.5 These inflammatory cytokines interfere with and actively inhibit the insulin signaling pathway within peripheral tissues, including skeletal muscles, the liver, and adipose cells, thereby exacerbating the existing insulin resistance.2 This establishes a self-amplifying positive feedback loop: visceral obesity leads to chronic inflammation, which in turn deepens insulin resistance, resulting in further metabolic dysfunction, such as elevated triglycerides (TG). This means that therapeutic strategies aiming to halt MS progression must prioritize addressing insulin resistance and chronic inflammation rather than focusing solely on managing downstream symptoms like elevated blood pressure or glucose levels. Intervention focusing on weight management is therefore a crucial mechanistic break point. The accumulated adverse effects of this chronic inflammatory state and resulting metabolic dysfunction manifest across several body systems.2 Specifically, endothelial dysfunction and hypertension, both consequential outcomes of MS, can ultimately lead to ischemic heart disease.1 Other non-core underlying risk factors that contribute to the development of MS include physical inactivity, aging, hormonal imbalance, and inherent genetic or ethnic predispositions.1

Diagnostic Criteria
The clinical diagnosis of Metabolic Syndrome relies on defined quantitative criteria, typically requiring the presence of any three out of five specific metabolic risk factors, to accurately predict future T2DM and CVD risk. The lack of a single, universally accepted definition results in diagnostic friction, as different criteria proposed by major organizations—such as the National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATP III), the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), and the Joint Interim Statement (JIS)—lead to varying prevalence estimates in populations.1 The NCEP-ATP III Criteria requires meeting 3 of the 5 criteria. These criteria include elevated waist circumference (as a measure of central obesity), elevated triglycerides (TG), reduced High-Density Lipoprotein Cholesterol (HDL-C), elevated blood pressure (BP), and elevated fasting glucose [1, 5].


The Challenge of Anthropometric Definitions in African Populations
A significant challenge in standardizing MS diagnosis globally is the application of anthropometric definitions across different ethnic groups. The established NCEP-ATP III and generalized IDF WC cut-offs, often derived from Caucasian populations, may inaccurately reflect the true visceral adiposity risk in African populations due to inherent differences in body composition, lean mass distribution, and fat patterning.8 Research attempting to establish African-specific criteria has highlighted considerable complexity and heterogeneity. One study, using the JIS criteria as a basis, found optimal Waist Circumference cut-off points for predicting the presence of at least two other MS components to be 86 cm for men (with a sensitivity of 61.2% and specificity of 82.9%) and 92 cm for women (with a sensitivity of 45.9% and specificity of 81.9%).7 However, this finding contrasts sharply with another analysis in a different cohort that suggested an optimal threshold of 80.3 cm for women aged 18–49 years, a value that aligns more closely with standard IDF guidance for Europids.8
This contradiction in optimal anthropometric thresholds—for example, 92 cm versus 80.3 cm for women—creates significant diagnostic friction and methodological bias when comparing regional prevalence studies. Without a consistent, validated measure of central obesity, prevalence tracking, resource allocation, and therapeutic guidelines based on visceral fat risk are scientifically compromised. Consequently, for reliable public health planning across the continent, there must be a unified, high-priority effort to establish large-scale, pan-African anthropometric criteria that accurately reflect regional body composition profiles.

Metabolic Syndrome in Africa Population
The continent of Africa faces a substantial and rapidly growing burden of Metabolic Syndrome. An analysis compiling 345 prevalence data points from 29 countries revealed that the overall pooled prevalence of MS in Africa is 32.4% (95% CI: 30.2–34.7).9 This statistic indicates that nearly one in every three adults across the continent is affected by this condition. Geographically, the burden is not uniformly distributed. The Southern African region was identified as the most affected sub-region, reporting a prevalence of 33.6% (95% CI: 28.3–39.1). This was closely followed by populations residing in upper-middle-income economies, suggesting that economic development and the resulting changes in diet and lifestyle are key risk modifiers.9 A defining characteristic of MS epidemiology in Africa is the strong female predominance. The pooled prevalence of MS in women across Africa is significantly higher at 36.9% (95% CI: 33.2–40.7), compared to men at 26.7% (95% CI: 23.1–30.5) ($P < 0.001$).9 This disparity is even more pronounced in specific regional studies; for example, in North-Western Nigeria, the prevalence was reported as 42.83% for females versus 27.36% for males.10 Analysis of the component profile reveals that African women frequently present with low HDL cholesterol (up to 62.2%) and central obesity (up to 49.8%) as the most prevalent components of the syndrome.10 The concentration of central obesity and low HDL in women suggests a unique combination of biological (hormonal factors, parity) and socioeconomic influences, such as changes in gendered societal roles, dietary habits associated with urbanization, and potential genetic predispositions for central fat deposition. This demographic pattern dictates that public health campaigns must be highly targeted and culturally sensitive, prioritizing weight and lipid profile interventions for women. Furthermore, the prevalence of MS shows a clear dependence on age. The overall pooled estimate for adults (Age > 18 years) was 33.1%, significantly higher than the 13.3% observed in children (0–18 years).9 This increase can be rapid, with some cohorts demonstrating prevalence rising from 11% in subjects aged 20–29 years to as high as 89% in those aged 70–79 years.4 Although prevalence continues to increase with age, the available data suggest that adults classified as middle-aged (40–60 years) are predominantly affected.4 Across the general population, the most common components observed in a study of North-Western Nigeria were low HDL (56.1%) and hypertension (46.1%).10 Conversely, while central obesity and low HDL were commoner in females, combinations involving high blood pressure and hypercholesterolemia often demonstrated male predominance.4
Pharmacological Management: Targets, Toxicity, and Therapeutic Efficacy of Orthodox Drugs 
Core Therapeutic Principle: Lifestyle First, Pharmacotherapy Second: The primary intervention for Metabolic Syndrome remains comprehensive lifestyle modification, including intentional weight loss, increased physical activity, and dietary change.1 Pharmacological management is introduced when the underlying metabolic risk factors fail to respond adequately to these non-drug interventions. The therapeutic strategy for MS is component-specific, focusing on independently managing atherogenic dyslipidemia, arterial hypertension, and hyperglycemia/insulin resistance.13
Orthodox Management of Atherogenic Dyslipidemia 
Atherogenic dyslipidemia, characterized by elevated triglycerides (TG), elevated apolipoprotein B, small LDL particles, and low HDL cholesterol (HDL-C), is a core risk factor.1
Statins: These 3-hydroxy-3-methyl-glutaryl-coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors represent the cornerstone of dyslipidemia management.13 They are crucial for lowering LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) and are available in various intensity regimens (low, moderate, and high) depending on the specific drug and dose (e.g., atorvastatin, simvastatin, rosuvastatin).13 While Statins are generally well-tolerated, but the primary clinical concern remains musculoskeletal risk, ranging from mild myalgia to, in rare cases, severe rhabdomyolysis.15
Non-Statin Therapies: These are often used when LDL-C goals are not met or in cases of statin intolerance. Ezetimibe works by inhibiting cholesterol absorption in the small intestine via the Niemann-Pick C1-Like-1 (NPC1L1) transporter.13 Fibrates (e.g., fenofibrate, gemfibrozil) and nicotinic acid are useful primarily for managing severe hypertriglyceridemia (TG $\ge 500$ mg/dL).13 Newer classes, such as Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) inhibitors (e.g., alirocumab, evolocumab), dramatically lower LDL-C by preventing the degradation of LDL receptors.13

Orthodox Management of Hyperglycemia and Insulin Resistance
The management of dysglycemia, particularly insulin resistance, is central to halting MS progression. Metformin is a primary therapeutic agent for addressing IR. It operates primarily by activating Adenosine Monophosphate-Activated Protein Kinase (AMPK), which subsequently reduces hepatic glucose production.14 While Metformin is highly valued for its efficacy in improving insulin sensitivity but is frequently associated with gastrointestinal intolerance (e.g., diarrhea, nausea), lactic acidosis (LA), and Vitamin B12 deficiency, linked to the malabsorption of Vitamin B12.17 This is connected to a decline in cognitive function and an increased chance of developing depressive symptoms.17 Nowadays, Incretin-Based Therapies that enhance insulin sensitization or augment postprandial insulin secretion are also available.12 Examples of the drugs include GLP-1 Receptor Agonists (GLP-1 RAs) and DPP-4 Inhibitors, which enhance insulin secretion, slowing gastric emptying, and promoting weight reduction.I It is worthy of note that DPP-4 Inhibitors typically achieve a two-fold increase in the postprandial plasma levels of endogenous GLP-1.12 Thiazolidinediones (TZDs) such as rosiglitazone, function as PPAR-gamma agonists, effectively increasing insulin sensitization in peripheral tissues.12

Orthodox Management of Arterial Hypertension
Hypertension is a highly prevalent component of MS in African cohorts, necessitating robust management strategies.10 Five major drug classes are available as first-line antihypertensive treatments: Thiazide/thiazide-like diuretics, Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs), Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors (ACEi), Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs), and Beta-blockers (BBs).13 Given the strong link between MS and cardio-renal risk, blockers of the Renin–Angiotensin System (RAS), specifically ACEi or ARBs, are often preferred and form a common component of general combination treatment strategies.13

Orthodox Management of Obesity
Drugs for obesity management include prescription medications like the gut-acting, gastric and pancreatic lipases inhibitor-orlistat, central acting phentermine-topiramate (Qsymia), naltrexone-(mu-Opioid receptor)-bupropion-(reuptake inhibitor of norepinephrine and dopamine) (Contrave), GLP-1 receptor agonism is the mechanism of action of liraglutide (Saxenda), semaglutide (Wegovy), and tirzepatide (Zepbound). These drugs work by targeting hormones to suppress appetite, reduce fat absorption, or increase feelings of fullness. Limitations include common side effects like nausea, vomiting, and dizziness, and the fact that some drugs may be limited to individuals with specific genetic disorders or pre-existing conditions like cardiovascular disease.  GLP-1 Receptor Agonists are effective weight loss and glucose-lowering agents, but their main side effects are highly prevalent gastrointestinal symptoms, including nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, and diarrhea.18 These effects do not usually lead to treatment discontinuation, and evidence suggests that long-acting formulations may be associated with less nausea than short-acting compounds.18 A critical, indirect toxicological risk is acute kidney injury (AKI). Severe GI symptoms can lead to dehydration, which, in turn, can cause pre-renal AKI.18 Therapeutic management in cases of severe overdose or persistent symptoms is primarily supportive, ensuring adequate fluid balance. The association between GLP-1 RAs and pancreatitis has been extensively debated

Orthodox Management of acute ischemic stroke
The main drug for treating an acute ischemic stroke is tissue plasminogen activator (tPA), a “clot-dissolving” medication that must be given within 3 to 4.5 hours of symptom onset to break up blood clots. After the acute phase, other drugs, including blood thinners (antiplatelets like aspirin, and anticoagulants) are prescribed for prevention, and medications to manage blood pressure, seizures, and muscle spasms. A major limitation for the use of tPA is not suitable for all patients due to the increased risk of severe bleeding especially in hemorrhagic stroke patients where tPA worsens bleeding.
